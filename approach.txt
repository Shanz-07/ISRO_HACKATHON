
                   reconstructing wavefront using SHWFS output image


OUTCOMES:

1. Reconstructed wavefront map (W(xi,yi))
2. Turbulence characteristics:
   -> i) r0 (coherence length) , ii) τ0 (coherence time)(t0 = 0.314 * r0/v0)
3. Actuator map (in terms of actuator stroke lengths taking into account the inter-actuator
   coupling)

What :

*When we try to observe a celestial body, the light reaching Earth can be approximated as a plane (flat) wavefront 
because the source is very far away. However, when it passes through the atmosphere, variations in temperature and 
density cause changes in refractive index.
These atmospheric turbulences distort the wavefront and we obtain a blurred or distorted image of the celestial body.

*The problem:
Due to atmospheric turbulence, the telescope receives a distorted wavefront and therefore cannot form a perfectly sharp image.



HOW:

*Scientists solved this using a Microlens Array (MLA) placed in a Shack-Hartmann Wavefront Sensor (SHWFS). 
The incoming distorted wavefront is incident on all the lenslets.
Each lenslet focuses the light onto a detector and forms a spot. 
The collection of all spot positions contains information about the local slopes of the incoming wavefront.

*The solution:
-> Initially we incident a plane wavefront of light onto the lenslets and the spot positions formed by each 
individual lenslet are recorded. These are our reference positions corresponding to an undistorted wavefront.

-> Now when we observe a celestial body, the distorted wavefront reaches the SHWFS and the focal spots shift 
from their reference positions by Δx and Δy.

-> We calculate the displacement (Δx, Δy) of every spot with respect to its reference position. 
These displacements are directly proportional to the local wavefront slopes (∂W/∂x and ∂W/∂y).

-> The spot centroids are calculated using centroiding algorithms and the centroid shifts are 
converted into local wavefront slope measurements.

-> These slope measurements from all lenslets are combined into a slope vector. 
Using wavefront reconstruction algorithms (Zernike modal reconstruction or least-squares reconstruction), 
the complete wavefront phase map W(x,y) is reconstructed.

-> Once the wavefront is reconstructed for every frame, turbulence statistics can be extracted from the phase data. 
The Fried parameter (r0) is estimated using Kolmogorov turbulence theory and phase structure functions
, while the coherence time (τ0) is estimated from the temporal evolution of the reconstructed wavefront.

-> To correct the distorted wavefront, a Deformable Mirror (DM) is used. 
The reconstructed wavefront error is converted into an equal and opposite correction wavefront.

-> Since each actuator on the deformable mirror influences not only its own region but also neighbouring regions 
(inter-actuator coupling), an influence matrix is used to model the mirror behaviour.

-> Using the reconstructed wavefront and the DM influence matrix, actuator stroke lengths are calculated. 
These stroke lengths represent how much each actuator must move to generate the corrective wavefront.

-> The final outputs are:
1) Reconstructed Wavefront Map W(xi,yi)
2) Turbulence Parameters (r0 and τ0)
3) Deformable Mirror Actuator Stroke Map(W=IA)


Why :

While the physics of wavefront reconstruction are well understood, 
implementing this in a real-time Adaptive Optics loop presents three severe engineering challenges:


The Latency Constraint:
Atmospheric turbulence evolves on a timescale characterized by τ0.The complete sensing,
reconstruction and correction pipeline must operate faster than this coherence time. Otherwise the calculated correction
corresponds to an outdated wavefront and becomes ineffective.


The Computational Load: Translating hundreds of spot deviations into a full wavefront phase map requires massive matrix 
multiplications. This demands highly optimized, low-level algorithms (like C) to ensure computational efficiency and prevent 
memory bottlenecks.

Mechanical Cross-Coupling: Deformable Mirror actuators are not physically independent; 
pushing one motor pulls the glass of its immediate neighbors. Systems that fail to calculate an "influence matrix" 
to decouple these forces will warp the mirror incorrectly and worsen the image.
